1. Pharyngitis

1.1Definition & Causes

  • Infection of the pharynx and/or tonsils
  • Age < 4 y ➞ mainly viral; > 4 y ➞ mainly bacterial

Viruses: 80% (most common)

  • Rhinovirus · coronavirus · adenovirus · enterovirus · influenza · parainfluenza · RSV · EBV · HSV
  • Cough, rhinitis, conjunctivitis ➞ think viral
  • Exudate can also be viral ➞ EBV, adenovirus

Bacteria: 20%

  • GABHS (15%) ➞ severe complications; scarlet fever (sandpaper) rash
  • Group C & G strep · C. diphtheriae · Arcanobacterium haemolyticum · Chlamydophila pneumoniae · Mycoplasma pneumoniae · Yersinia enterocolitica · Francisella tularensis

1. Pharyngitis (continued)

1.5Should We Treat GAS? & Clinical Approach

  • Yes ➞ to prevent acute rheumatic fever (9-day window)
  • Antibiotics do NOT prevent glomerulonephritis
  • Child looks well ➞ RADT: (+) ➞ start Tx; (−) ➞ wait for culture
  • No RADT/culture available ➞ decide clinically (epidemiology + manifestations)

1.6Complications of GAS Pharyngitis

1.7Treatment

  • Penicillin V ➞ GAS highly susceptible
  • Alternative: amoxicillin ➞ 10 days
  • β-lactam allergy: macrolide or clindamycin ➞ in severe/disseminated disease, confirm sensitivity first

1.8Lecture Cases

Case 1: 3 y, low-grade fever, runny nose, cough, sore throat

  • Age ≤ 3 y + cough + rhinitis ➞ viral ➞ supportive
  • Tonsillar swelling + headache + unilateral tender cervical LN ➞ would point to bacterial

Case 2: 6 y, high fever, severe sore throat, no cough

  • Age > 3 y + exudate + no cough ➞ GAS (± scarlet fever)
  • ➞ antibiotic (amoxicillin 10 d) to prevent rheumatic fever

2. Acute Otitis Media (AOM)

2.1Etiology

  • Bacteria 60–80% ➞ why we treat
    • S. pneumoniae 30% = #1 despite vaccine (vaccine covers only invasive subtypes)
    • H. influenzae 20% ➞ often amoxicillin-resistant ➞ amox-clav; now ↓ by vaccination
    • M. catarrhalis 20%
  • Viruses 20–40%
  • Spontaneous resolution 50% (> 2 y)
  • Purulent conjunctivitis ➞ think H. influenzae

2.2Pathophysiology

Viral URTI ➞ secretions + edema ➞ Eustachian tube (ET) dysfunction = hallmark
▸ ET blocked ➞ −ve middle-ear pressure ➞ reflux of nasopharyngeal microbes into ME
▸ ↓ mucociliary defense · ↑ bacterial colonization & adhesion · altered immunity
▸ Nose blowing / sniffing ➞ pushes microbes into ME
▸ Mucus accumulates in ME ➞ medium for bacterial growth
▸ Adenoids ➞ bacterial reservoir?
➞ Ascending bacterial (± viral) infection via ET ➞ AOM

2.3Clinical Presentation

Symptoms

  • Ear pain (ear tugging in infants)
  • Irritability / crying
  • Fever
  • Ear discharge (± perforation)
  • Diarrhea ± vomiting

Signs

  • Bulging TM ➞ hallmark
  • Dull TM
  • Absent light reflex
  • ↓ / absent mobility
  • Air-fluid level
  • Otorrhea
  • Bulging ➞ only in acute suppurative OM; redness alone ➞ not specific

2.4Who to Treat & For How Long

2.6Case 3

  • 6-mo infant: fever, vomiting, excessive crying
  • Ear exam: bulging TM ➞ AOM
  • < 2 y ➞ antibiotics now, 10 d

2.7Complications

  • TM perforation · conductive hearing loss · mastoiditis · labyrinthitis
  • Brain abscess · epidural abscess
  • Otic hydrocephalus ➞ ↑ ICP without hydrocephalus

3. Acute Bacterial Sinusitis (ABS)

3.1Pathogenesis

  • ABS ➞ 100% preceded by URTI (AOM ➞ ~90%)
  • Sinus mucosa is continuous with nasal mucosa
  • < 4 y ➞ no sinusitis (sinuses underdeveloped)

3 keys to normal sinus physiology

  1. Patent ostia ➞ URTI secretions block the ostiomeatal complex
  2. Ciliary function ➞ moves secretions to the nose; impaired (congenital, CF, acute URTI) ➞ bacteria multiply
  3. Secretion quality ➞ overproduction in URTI
  • ➞ Fluid accumulation ➞ medium for bacteria ➞ sinusitis
  • High risk: damaged cilia (e.g. cystic fibrosis)

3.2Clinical Manifestations (3 Types)

PersistentNasal discharge and/or cough > 10 d, not improving
SevereT ≥ 39 °C + purulent nasal discharge together > 3 d
WorseningURTI improving ➞ worse on day 6–7: new/recurrent fever or ↑ nasal symptoms/cough

3.3Etiology

  • S. pneumoniae 30–40% · H. influenzae 20% · M. catarrhalis 20% · viruses 10%
  • Staphylococci, anaerobes, fungi ➞ uncommon; seen in immunocompromised

3.4Diagnosis

  • Clinical is enough
  • Uncomplicated ➞ no imaging (only finding = mucosal thickening, also seen in viral URTI)
  • Sinus aspiration ➞ special cases only: not responding to Tx, immunocompromised

3.5Who to Treat & Management (Flowchart)

Treat now if any:
▸ Severe or worsening symptoms
▸ Concurrent bacterial infection (AOM, pneumonia)
▸ Suspected complications
▸ Underlying disease: asthma, CF, ciliary dysfunction, immunodeficiency
None ➞ outpatient observation 3 d ➞ improving: symptomatic · not improving: treat
Choosing the antibiotic
▸ Daycare, severe, or amoxicillin in last 30 d ➞ high-dose amox-clav
▸ None of these ➞ high-dose amoxicillin ➞ not improving ➞ amox-clav
▸ Improving ➞ continue until 7 d after symptoms resolve (total 7–10 d)
▸ Not improving on amox-clav ➞ sinus imaging / aspiration (fungal in immunocompromised) ➞ adjust per C/S

3.6Complication

  • Brain abscess

4. Skin & Soft Tissue Infections (SSTI) ➞ mainly S. aureus + group A strep

4.1Case 4 & Impetigo

  • Case 4: 6 y, red swollen chin + golden crusts ➞ impetigo
    • Well & afebrile ➞ topical antibiotic (mupirocin)
    • Ill & febrile ➞ systemic (narrow-spectrum, e.g. flucloxacillin)
  • Localized, highly contagious; age 2–5 y
  • Non-bullous ➞ GAS ➞ golden/honey crusts
  • Bullous ➞ S. aureus toxin (not the organism) ➞ pustules rupture ➞ erosion with collarette of scale ➞ heals without scar

4.2Folliculitis

  • Hair follicle, epidermis only ➞ more superficial & widespread than furuncle
  • Discrete 2–5 mm papules/pustules on red base; single or grouped; hair shaft in center
  • S. aureus (predominant) ± GAS
  • Often resolves spontaneously, no scar
  • Mild ➞ chlorhexidine cleanser, topical mupirocin/clindamycin, benzoyl peroxide

4.3Furuncle & Carbuncle

  • Furuncle (boil): hair follicle infection extending deep into dermis
  • Carbuncle: painful aggregate of contiguous follicles, multiple drainage points, surrounding inflammation
  • Almost always S. aureus
  • Tx: frequent hot, moist compress ➞ drainage; large furuncles & most carbuncles ➞ surgical drainage

4.4Ecthyma & Ecthyma Gangrenosum

  • Ecthyma: rare, deep ulcer into dermis ➞ S. pyogenes ➞ systemic anti-streptococcal antibiotic
  • Ecthyma gangrenosum: necrotic center, serious, toxin-mediated ➞ gram −ve, esp. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
    • Immunocompromised / neutropenic (e.g. leukemia) ➞ septicemia, can be fatal
    • ➞ Prompt anti-pseudomonal + blood culture

4.5Erysipelas vs Cellulitis

5. Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

5.1Definition & Case 5

  • UTI = significant bacteriuria + symptoms
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria ➞ don't treat (except pregnancy / before procedures)
  • Infants: fever with no focus is often the only sign
  • Case 5: 9 mo, fever 3 d, well, T 38.9 °C, no focus ➞ urine analysis
  • Suspect UTI: fever ≥ 38 °C + no source, esp. with age < 12 mo, female, uncircumcised boy, T > 39 °C, fever > 48 h

5.2Pathogenesis

  • GI bacteria colonize periurethral mucosa ➞ ascend ➞ bladder ➞ ureters ➞ kidneys
  • Bacterial virulence ➞ e.g. Klebsiella adheres to ureter ➞ kidney
  • Host: obstruction + urostasis (stones, tumor, constipation), neurogenic bladder, VUR
  • Circumcision ↓ UTI ~8× in boys < 3 mo

5.3Etiology

  • E. coli 70–80% (from anal area) · Klebsiella · Proteus · Pseudomonas · Enterococcus (gram +) · Enterobacter
  • GBS ➞ neonates
  • S. saprophyticus ➞ adolescent girls (gram +); S. aureus rare
  • Candida ➞ urinary catheter ➞ remove catheter
  • Adenovirus ➞ hemorrhagic cystitis (immunocompromised)
  • TB ➞ sterile pyuria (↑ WBC/pus, culture −ve)
  • Saudi data: E. coli + K. pneumoniae most common; resistance ampicillin ~70%, co-trimoxazole ~60% ➞ 3rd-gen cephalosporin / aminoglycoside

5.7Diagnostic Approach (Flowchart)

Suspect UTI: infant with fever + no focus, or older child with urinary symptoms
➞ Collect by recommended method ➞ dipstick + microscopy + culture
▸ Febrile with urinary symptoms / unwell ➞ start empiric antibiotic now ➞ adjust per culture
▸ Afebrile with urinary symptoms / well with fever, no source ➞ check dipstick + microscopy:
LE, nitrite, pyuria or bacteriuria (+) ➞ start empiric antibiotic, await culture
All (−) ➞ wait for culture; repeat after 24–48 h if fever persists

5.8Treatment

  • Toxic or can't take oral ➞ IV until improved for 24–48 h ➞ switch to oral
  • Empiric ➞ then step down per culture; check previous cultures

5.9Imaging

First febrile UTI, < 3 y ➞ RBUS 2–6 wk after infection (look for anomalies) ➞ abnormal ➞ VCUG
Any age + recurrent / complicated / febrile non-E. coli UTI / family history of VUR ➞ RBUS + VCUG ➞ abnormal ➞ refer urology/nephrology
  • Why wait 2–6 wk? Acute inflammation / debris ➞ false-positive US
  • Early US ➞ severely ill, complicated, obstruction, or not improving by 48 h ➞ detects renal/perirenal abscess, pyonephrosis
  • Recurrent = ≥ 2 symptomatic UTIs in 12 mo
  • Complicated = critically ill (urosepsis/shock), obstruction, or no response after 48 h of antibiotics
  • Refer ➞ recurrent UTI + abnormal imaging, VUR grade III–IV, congenital renal anomaly, bladder dysfunction, ↓ kidney function, hypertension

Case-Based Practice MCQs (1–5 = lecture cases; answers on the next page)

1. 3-year-old: low-grade fever, runny nose, cough, sore throat. Best management?

  1. Penicillin V for 10 days
  2. Supportive treatment
  3. RADT, amoxicillin if positive
  4. Clindamycin

2. 6-year-old: high fever, severe sore throat, no cough, tonsillar exudate. Main reason to give antibiotics?

  1. Shorten the illness
  2. Prevent acute rheumatic fever
  3. Prevent sinusitis only
  4. Treat EBV

3. 6-month-old: fever, vomiting, excessive crying; ear exam shows a bulging TM. Best management?

  1. Observe 48–72 h
  2. Antibiotics now, 10 days
  3. Antibiotics, 5 days
  4. Tympanocentesis first

4. 6-year-old: red swollen chin with golden crusts; well and afebrile. Best treatment?

  1. Topical mupirocin
  2. IV flucloxacillin
  3. Surgical drainage
  4. Anti-pseudomonal antibiotic

5. 9-month-old: fever 3 days, looks well, T 38.9 °C, no focus. Next step?

  1. Chest X-ray
  2. Urine analysis
  3. Throat culture
  4. Reassure and discharge

6. Rapid antigen test for GAS: which is correct?

  1. Sensitivity 97%, so (−) excludes GAS
  2. Specificity > 95%, lower sensitivity ➞ (−) needs culture
  3. Takes 2–3 days
  4. More accurate than culture

7. 3-year-old with AOM took amoxicillin 3 weeks ago. First-line now?

  1. High-dose amoxicillin
  2. High-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate
  3. IV ceftriaxone
  4. Azithromycin

8. Hallmark sign of AOM?

  1. Red TM
  2. Bulging TM
  3. Ear tugging
  4. Fever

9. Nasal discharge and cough for 12 days, not improving, afebrile. Diagnosis?

  1. Viral URTI, reassure
  2. Persistent acute bacterial sinusitis
  3. Severe acute bacterial sinusitis
  4. Needs sinus CT

10. Neutropenic child with leukemia: skin lesion with necrotic black center. Organism?

  1. S. aureus
  2. S. pyogenes
  3. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
  4. Group B strep

11. 10-month-old, not toilet-trained. Collection method and cut-off?

  1. Urine bag, any growth
  2. Transurethral catheter, > 50,000 CFU/mL
  3. Midstream, > 100,000 CFU/mL
  4. Suprapubic, > 100,000 CFU/mL

12. 4-year-old with febrile UTI, well, drinking. Correct statement?

  1. IV beats oral
  2. Oral = IV in children ≥ 2 months
  3. Ampicillin preferred in Saudi children
  4. Co-trimoxazole is first line

13. 1-year-old recovered from first febrile UTI. When is RBUS done?

  1. Immediately, during fever
  2. 2–6 weeks after infection
  3. Only after a 2nd UTI
  4. Never under 3 years

14. Pyuria with repeatedly sterile cultures. Consider?

  1. E. coli
  2. Adenovirus
  3. Tuberculosis
  4. Candida
